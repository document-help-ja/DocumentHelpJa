Passports (PICA) All Services How It Works
PICA · RGD · Complete Guide

Jamaican passports and citizenship by descent, explained in plain language.

Every requirement below comes from PICA's and the RGD's own published rules — organized so you can find your situation, check what's required, and avoid the exact mistakes that cause rejections.

Section 1 · Step 0

The RGD Prerequisite

Before PICA will accept any application, your civil registry documents have to clear one gate first: the Registrar General's Department.

Step 0 — Confirm this before you do anything else

PICA does not independently verify births, adoptions, or marriages. It relies entirely on the RGD — Jamaica's sole repository for birth, marriage, stillbirth, and death records since 1879 — and its systems are integrated directly with RGD's database. Hospital records, baptismal certificates, and foreign notarized copies will not be accepted as substitutes. Attempting to use them is the single leading cause of application rejection.

Is your birth certificate the accepted format?

On May 7, 2001, the RGD moved from handwritten civil documents to computer-generated certificates on controlled security paper. In February 2023, the security paper itself was upgraded again, adding more than twelve tamper-proof features. PICA enforces this transition strictly — the older the document format, the higher the rejection risk.

  • Handwritten, black-and-white certificate: obsolete since 2002 — will be categorically rejected.
  • "Pink slip" (Certificate of Registry): legally only evidence of registration, not a valid birth certificate — will be rejected.
  • Modern RGD security paper with deeply engraved "blind" embossing, a visible watermark, and computer-generated text: meets the current threshold — accepted.
  • Post-February 2023 enhanced security paper: the highest current level of compliance.
  • Post-2001 certificate with severe wear, tear, or fading: PICA officers have discretionary authority to reject it — even though the RGD states issued certificates never expire.

If your certificate doesn't meet the modern standard, the fix is to apply to the RGD for a replacement computer-generated certificate. You'll need the Birth Entry Number, the certificate holder's full name, both parents' names, date and place of birth, and sex.

Official web portals for document retrieval
  • Registrar General's Department (RGD): www.rgd.gov.jm
  • National Identification and Registration Authority (NIRA): www.nira.gov.jm
Section 2

Adult Passport Renewal: Online vs. Consulate

There are two completely different pathways, and the eligibility rules for the faster one are unforgiving. One deviation triggers an automatic system lockout into the manual route.

01

Online Renewal — 3 Strict Rules

All three must be true, with no exceptions, or the portal locks you out:

  • Age Rule: must be an adult renewing a previously issued adult passport — the system can't handle minor consent verification.
  • Issue Date Rule: the expiring passport must have been issued after September 2001 (Machine-Readable Passports only), and must be expired or expiring within a year.
  • Exact Name Rule: your current legal name must identically match the name on the expiring passport. Any name change from marriage, divorce, or Deed Poll disqualifies you from online renewal entirely.

What you'll need: a computer, valid email, the expiring passport, a digital JPEG passport photo, a PDF reader, and a Visa or Mastercard.

Official PICA online renewal portal →
02

Consulate / Mail-In Renewal

Mandatory if you have a name change, hold a pre-2001 passport, or are renewing a minor's passport. Incomplete applications are routinely returned at your expense.

  • Completed application form on A4/letter paper — Simplified Adult Renewal Form if no name change, otherwise the regular form.
  • Original expired passport — surrendered, canceled, and returned with the new one.
  • Original RGD civil documents — birth, adoption, or citizenship certificate, modern security-paper format only.
  • Valid government photo ID (Green Card, foreign passport, foreign driver's license), or a Declaration of Identity (DOI) form if you lack one.
  • Two identical passport photographs, one formally certified.
  • Legal name-change documentation if applicable — Marriage Certificate, Divorce Judgement (Decree Absolute), or a registered Deed Poll.
Timelines & Fees

Processing Times (2026 Baseline)

  • Local — Kingston Head Office: 7 working days (Regular Service).
  • Local — Regional outposts (Montego Bay, May Pen): 11–14 working days (Regular Service).
  • Overseas — Consulates/Embassies: officially 20 working days after receipt in Kingston; real-world, accounting for diplomatic pouch transit, expect 4–8 weeks.
  • Expedited service: local only. Kingston offers Same-Day, Next-Day, and 3-Day. Outposts offer 5-Day and 7-Day. There is no expedited option for overseas diaspora applicants.

Section 3

The Danger Zone: Top Reasons for Rejection

PICA's OCR and biometric cropping software has zero tolerance for visual anomalies. Three areas cause almost every rejection: the photo, the signature box, and unauthorized certifiers.

Photo specifications — the contrast rule

The software maps facial geometry for the e-passport microchip, not just a visual check. Photos must be less than six months old, printed in color with a matte/dull finish, with no shadows, creases, ink marks, or embossed seals. Headgear is only permitted for documented religious purposes, with Section H of the form completed.

Dimensional constraints — the most common failure point

Face length (chin to forehead) must be exactly 25mm–35mm. When cropped, there must be a 3mm–4mm margin between the top of the head and the edge of the photo.

The biometric contrast rule:

  • Dark complexion: wear brightly colored clothing, photographed against a plain white background.
  • Light complexion: avoid white clothing; use a pastel-shaded background so the face doesn't blend in.
  • Grey or white hair: use a pale blue or pastel background for geometric separation from the hair.
  • Glasses: lenses cannot be tinted, frames cannot be thick/heavy, and there must be no flash reflection on the lens.
The signature box — a zero-tolerance border rule

The trap

If your signature touches, overlaps, or breaches the printed black border of the signature box on Page 2, the application is instantly rejected. The scanner reads the border line as part of the signature and corrupts the digital file.

This box is electronically scanned, and its contents are laser-engraved onto the polycarbonate data page of the e-passport. Sign smoothly, centered within the box, and keep it closely identical to the secondary signature required in Section E on the same page.

Who can certify your application (overseas)

If you're applying from the diaspora, only these professionals may certify your application:

  • A Consular Officer (at a Jamaican Embassy or High Commission)
  • A Justice of the Peace (JP)
  • A Notary Public
  • A Commissioner of Oaths
  • A Medical Doctor
  • An Attorney-at-Law / Lawyer
  • A Minister of Religion authorized to perform marriages

What the certifier must do: physically complete Section G of the form and endorse one — and only one — passport photograph. On the back of that photo, they write the applicant's name, add their own signature, provide their registration/stamp number, and date the certification.

Two more traps

If the certifier hasn't known you personally for over 12 months, they're legally required to note that you were "seen in person" and verified via secondary ID. And any certification older than six months automatically voids the entire application.

Section 4

Citizenship by Descent: Claiming Heritage

Citizenship by descent secures unconditional landing rights, a Jamaican passport, and the right to own property and work without restriction. The evidentiary burden, however, is substantial.

Pre-Independence Births

Born outside Jamaica before August 6, 1962: you qualify if your mother or father became — or would have become, but for their death — a citizen of Jamaica on August 6, 1962.

Post-Independence Births

Born outside Jamaica after August 5, 1962: you qualify if your mother or father was a citizen of Jamaica (by birth, descent, or registration via marriage) at the exact date of your birth.

The exact document checklist

Only original documents are accepted. Photocopies — even heavily notarized ones — are categorically rejected.

  • Your foreign birth certificate, listing both parents' names.
  • The Jamaican parent's RGD civil document — birth certificate, Certificate of Jamaican Citizenship, or adoption certificate, in the modern security format.
  • Marriage certificates, if your parents were married at the time of your birth, or if you're a married woman using your husband's surname.
  • Your valid government ID — a driver's license or foreign passport if you're an adult; the Jamaican parent's valid ID if you're a minor.
  • Two passport-sized photographs, meeting the same biometric contrast rules from Section 3, with one certified by a Notary Public, JP, or Consular Officer.

The "Name Discrepancy" trap

Many Jamaican immigrants (including the Windrush generation) anglicized names or altered spellings without a formal legal name change. PICA cross-references the parent's name on the foreign birth certificate against the RGD birth certificate — and treats even a missing middle name or a single misspelled letter as two separate legal identities, halting the application.

The fix: an Official Change of Name Document. The parent, if living, must retroactively execute a Deed Poll through the foreign court system or Records Office to legally bridge the two identities. An affidavit alone is not sufficient.

One exception: if there's no father's information on the Jamaican birth certificate and no surname was given to the child, PICA accepts the document under the doctrine that the child assumes the mother's maiden name.

Timelines & Fees

Fees and Estimated Processing Times

Officially, compliant applications process within 90 working days (about 4.5 months) for Regular Service, or 45 working days Expedited. In practice, diaspora applicants should expect 6 to 12 months once the embassy dispatches the diplomatic pouch, due to backlogs and mail routing.

Section 5

Minor Passports (Under 18)

The state treats issuing a travel document to a minor as a child-protection matter, and the requirements shift the verification burden entirely onto the parent or legal guardian. A minor cannot apply independently, unless they're an active member of the Security Forces.

Parental ID & signature matching (Sections C & E)

The mother, father (if legally named on the birth certificate), or a court-appointed legal guardian must complete and sign Section C (Consent) and Section E (Declaration).

The trap

Signatures on Sections C and E must be closely identical to each other, and must match the signature on the parent's valid government-issued photo ID submitted with the application. Any discrepancy in penmanship triggers rejection.

Who gets certified — the parent, not the child

Section G certification verifies the identity and application rights of the parent or guardian applying — not the minor.

The minor's signature rule — age 12 threshold
  • Under 12 years old: no signature is required in the rectangular box — leave it blank.
  • 12 years or older: the minor must sign within the box, following the same border rule from Section 3 — no touching the edges.
Mandatory physical appearance — age 3 threshold

If the minor is three years of age or older, they must appear in person alongside the consenting parent or legal guardian when the application is submitted at a PICA location or overseas mission.

Legal guardianship & name changes

If the applying adult's current legal name differs from the name on the minor's RGD birth certificate, proof of the name change (Marriage Certificate, Divorce Decree, or Court Order) is required.

If the applicant is a legal guardian rather than a biological parent, a formal court-issued guardianship document is required — informal consent letters are not accepted. At a consulate, guardians may instead submit a care order, or a notarized letter of consent from the biological Jamaican parent along with that parent's ID.
